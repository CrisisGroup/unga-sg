Multiplying Crises, Fraying Consensus at the 2026 UN General Assembly

World leaders discussed an array of peace and security issues during their speeches at the 2026 UN General Assembly. Crisis Group experts analyse the General Debate and offer insights about the competing priorities of UN members.

by the INTERNATIONAL CRISIS GROUP

Published TK October, 2026

Last month representatives from over 190 states gathered in New York for the UN General Assembly’s annual High-Level Week, grappling with an organisation in need of revival and paralysed on a range of geopolitical crises. Many of the 67 presidents, 41 prime ministers, five crowned royals, and 79 other dignitaries who spoke during the General Debate focused their remarks on domestic concerns – from climate change, to rising energy prices, to the potential risks of artificial intelligence. But their speeches to the Assembly also provided a helpful illustration of which conflicts are at the top of UN members minds.

Crisis Group has analysed the 192 speeches delivered during the General Debate, focusing on four geographic crises: Palestine, Iran and the Strait of Hormuz, Ukraine, and Sudan, as well as UN-specific issues related to institutional reforms and the process to select the next Secretary-General.

Palestine

The Occupied Palestinian Territories was the most referenced geographic crisis during the General Debate, with 98 speakers highlighting the situation in Gaza or Palestine more broadly. Following Israel’s recent, more aggressive pursuit of its settlement agenda, 37 of those speakers – including both Israel and the State of Palestine – also spotlighted the former’s actions in the West Bank, though Israel focused on defending settler violence more broadly as the actions of “a handful of juvenile delinquents.”

  • Referred to Gaza or Palestine, and the West Bank (37)
  • Referred to Gaza or Palestine only (61)
  • Did not refer to Gaza or Palestine (94)

Despite remaining the most discussed geographic crisis at the UN – with over half of the membership speaking on the topic – there was a 35 per cent decrease in the number of references compared to 2025. This is illustrative of a wider trend at the UN of a declining focus on the Palestinian issue since the adoption of the Security Council’s November 2025 resolution endorsing the Trump administration’s Gaza ceasefire plan and the creation of the Board of Peace. The crises in Gaza and the West Bank did not garner as much attention at the 2026 General Debate as they did in 2025, when a handful of Western countries made significant announcements on their recognition of Palestinian statehood on the back of a joint French-Saudi-led Conference on the Two State Solution. Member states and civil society organisations did convene multiple side events on the margins of High-Level Week – including a ministerial gathering of the Global Alliance for the Implementation of the Two-State Solution, to help keep the issue high on the diplomatic radar.

  • Referred to Gaza or Palestine, and the West Bank (37)
  • Referred to Gaza or Palestine only (61)
  • Did not refer to Gaza or Palestine (94)

Iran

This year 64 speakers – over one third of the UN’s membership – explicitly mentioned the conflict in Iran or the Strait of Hormuz. That number, however, does not include countries indirect references to the broader crisis in the Middle East, the importance of freedom of navigation as a matter of international law, or other states’ concerns over President Trump’s threat to ‘annihilate’ Iran. It also does not take into account delegations that highlighted the strains on global supply chains and energy prices – a key domestic concern for many – without explicitly referencing either Iran or the Strait of Hormuz. With various diplomatic efforts on the war taking place throughout High-Level Week, including brief discussions between American and Iranian diplomats, some UN members may have preferred to make only generalised remarks about the crisis.

  • Referred to Iran (42)
  • Referred to the Strait of Hormuz only (22)
  • Did not refer to Iran or the Strait of Hormuz (128)

Ukraine

There continued to be a downward trend in the UN’s focus on Ukraine, with only 76 countries mentioning Ukraine in their speeches, a significant decrease from the 106 that referred to Ukraine in 2025. Among the 76 speeches, nearly a third of them did so without explicitly mentioning Russia as a party to – and aggressor of – the conflict. Notably, this was the first year since Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 that less than half of the UN membership spoke about the ongoing war.

  • Referred to Ukraine and Russia (51)
  • Referred to Ukraine only (25)
  • Did not refer to Ukraine (116)

Of the 76 countries that referenced Ukraine, 40 of them were European countries, reinforcing the multi-year trend of Ukraine shifting away from a global concern to more of a regional concern. Whilst most who discussed the war did so in support of Kyiv’s sovereignty and territorial integrity, Niger and Mali – two of the three Sahelian states who jointly accused Ukraine of supporting terrorism in their region in 2024 – used their speeches to reiterate their claims against Ukraine for alleged attempts to destabilise the Sahel.

  • Referred to Ukraine and Russia (51)
  • Referred to Ukraine only (25)
  • Did not refer to Ukraine (116)

A similar trend emerged during the annual Security Council meeting on Ukraine that took place during High-Level Week. Eighteen states sent high-level officials (heads of government or state, or ministers) to participate in the Council meeting. With the exceptions of Bahrain and Panama, all were from European countries. Notably absent however was Ukrainian President Zelenskyy, who despite speaking at the General Assembly that same morning chose to forgo an appearance at the Security Council. After years of participating in this annual session with few tangible outcomes, Zelenskyy may have concluded that it has little value in his country’s efforts to repel Russia’s aggression.

  • Referred to Ukraine and Russia (51)
  • Referred to Ukraine only (25)
  • Did not refer to Ukraine (116)

Sudan

Among the four major geographic conflicts under the spotlight of this year’s General Debate, Sudan remains the least referenced. Despite the conflict remaining the world’s largest humanitarian crisis, only 63 countries mentioned the war in their 2026 speeches, one more state than in 2025.

Member states did host numerous side events on Sudan, including the first in-person meeting of the Quad (Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States) and the Quintet (African Union, European Union, Intergovernmental Authority on Development, League of Arab States, and United Nations) – two separate, but parallel groupings of states and multilateral organisations working to end the war in Sudan. However, these efforts were overshadowed by the Trump Administration’s decision to reject the visa application for Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, Chairperson of Sudan’s Transitional Sovereignty Council.

  • Referred to Sudan (63)
  • Did not refer to Sudan (129)

UN Reform

The need for institutional reform of the UN continued to be a popular topic of discussion, with 122 countries referencing it in their general debate speeches, only six fewer than 2025. Discussion of UN reform is split primarily into two tracks: UN80, the initiative launched last year by Secretary-General Guterres to reform the organisation through a combination of financial cuts, a review of its mandates, and structural changes; and Security Council reform, a longstanding effort by UN members to update the Council’s composition and veto powers. While the number of countries raising Council reform (104) remained similar to 2025, there was a 35 per cent decrease in those who explicitly discussed UN80, raising the question of whether momentum and support for the initiative will continue past Guterres’ term, ending this year.

  • Referred to UN80 and Security Council Reform (29)
  • Referred to UN80 only (18)
  • Referred to Security Council reform only (75)
  • Did not refer to UN reform (70)

Next Secretary-General

Amidst the fanfare of High-Level Week, the question of who will succeed Secretary-General Guterres loomed over the proceedings and dominated many of the side events organised by civil society. However, 63 countries, fewer than one third of speakers, used their remarks to share what they would like to see from the organisation’s next leader. This may be a symptom of the little say the wider membership has in the selection process itself – where the fifteen members of the Security Council vote to select a candidate to recommend to the General Assembly – as opposed to a lack of interest in what the next Secretary-General should prioritise.

  • Referred to the next Secretary-General (63)
  • Did not refer to the next Secretary-General (129)

Conclusion

While many have identified the need for the next Secretary-General to take a more proactive approach to peacemaking, it was notable that world leaders used their General Debate speeches less to discuss global concerns of war and peace than in years past – some of which may be miles from home – and used their time instead to speak about national priorities and broader concerns related to international cooperation and multilateralism.

The lack of a clear, single geopolitical crisis dominating the UN discourse this year, however, also points to the multitude of active conflicts on the world agenda, the highest since World War II. The vast diversity of focus of the 192 speeches is illustrative of the many competing priorities that UN members are confronting, with no clear consensus on which of the many crises they want the UN to prioritise. The next Secretary-General will therefore inherit the difficult task of choosing where to direct the organisation’s attention and efforts, when its financial and political resources are increasingly limited.
